# Two clerical houses built one Dadar family

The Qaalluu and the Sharifa of Dadar are best understood not as "one Oromo clan and one Arab clan" but as **two Muslim clerical lineages that intermarried inside Oromo society**, and the written record supports that framing far more than it supports any specific claim about Ali Shabel or Sharif Abdalla, whose names appear in no published source found. "Qallu/Qaalluu" is firmly documented as a clan (gosa) of the Ittu Oromo: the *Encyclopaedia Aethiopica* places it among the six clans of the Ittu's Galaan confederacy, and independent lists agree ([EAe "Ittuu" via habeshahistory](https://www.habeshahistory.com/encyclopedia/ittuu-p0272-b-y1277-v3); [Afendi Muteki](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-oromo-of-harerghe-on-their-sub.html)). Its character is religious: Braukämper, as relayed by Wikipedia, describes the Warra Qallu among the Ittu as Somali-origin Muslim missionaries who became Oromo-speaking sheikhs while "preserving the memory of their Somali origin," and a Hararghe Oromo tradition recorded in a Jimma University thesis has the Warra Qallu adopt a sharif sent from Harar as their "ilma gosaa" ([Wikipedia: Ituu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ituu); [Admasu Feleke, Jimma 2014](https://repository.ju.edu.et/bitstream/handle/123456789/1171/MAIN%20BODY%20OF%20ADMASU'S%20THESIS1.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)). Sharif lineages in Hararghe are documented only as a type, never as the Sharifa of Burqa and Ganda Ali: the pattern of a holy man marrying a clan leader's daughter, being adopted into the clan, and founding a patrilineal "house" that keeps "Sharif" as its label is attested for Harar, Gelemso and Arsi, which is exactly the shape of the Sharif Abdalla–Ammo Ali Shabel story. The dense cross-marriage in the notebook is licit by both codes that governed these families: Oromo custom forbids marrying within one's own gosa but welcomes repeated exchange between two allied gosa, while Islamic law permits the cousin marriages that Oromo tradition does not. Generation counting places Ali Shabel's adult life in the middle decades of the nineteenth century, so the founders belong to the era of Egyptian occupation, Menelik's conquest and the Ittu's mass conversion, not to a medieval past; elders born in the 1930s–1950s knew his grandchildren. No state kept a name-level register of rural Hararghe Muslims before kebele land certification began around 2004 and civil registration in August 2016, so the book must rest on oral silsila, local Arabic genealogical manuscripts, and systematic interviews, with the Dadar and Doba woreda offices as the only paper sources worth visiting ([UNECA CRVS report](https://apai-crvs.uneca.org/sites/default/files/resourcefiles/Country%20Report%20-%20Ethiopia_Eng_0.pdf); [UN Ethiopia 2016](https://ethiopia.un.org/en/14847-vital-events-registration-kicks-ethiopia)).

A note on evidence labels used throughout. **Documented** means a published academic source was read directly or is quoted by page through a secondary source; **oral tradition** means a tradition reported by a local writer (chiefly Afendi Muteki's blog) or by the family notebook; **Wikipedia-only** means a Wikipedia statement carrying no citation; **inference** means the report writer's own reading, which no source states.

## The Qaalluu are a recognised Ittu clan whose name means "the priests"

Three independent sources count Qallu/Qaalluu as a clan of the Ittu Oromo of Chercher (western Hararghe). Mohammed Hassen's *Encyclopaedia Aethiopica* entry says the Ittu "developed into two confederacies, Galaan (consisting of six gosa or clans: Alga, Baabbo, Elle, Gaamo, Gaadulla and Qallu)" and "Kura (with four gosa: Addayye, Arrooǧǧii, Bayy and Waayyu)," and it fixes "the traditional boundary between the I[ttu] and Afran Qallo" at **the Burqa river** (documented; [EAe "Ittuu"](https://www.habeshahistory.com/encyclopedia/ittuu-p0272-b-y1277-v3)). Afendi Muteki's popular history lists ten Ittu clans ending with Qaalluu and repeats the Burqa-river boundary "near Baroodaa town" (oral tradition; [Afendi Muteki](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-oromo-of-harerghe-on-their-sub.html)), and a Jimma University MA thesis on Odaa Bultum calls the Warra Qallu "one of the ten clans that make up the Ittu Oromo" (documented; [Admasu Feleke 2014](https://repository.ju.edu.et/bitstream/handle/123456789/1171/MAIN%20BODY%20OF%20ADMASU'S%20THESIS1.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)). The one dissent is the English Wikipedia "Ituu" article, whose ten-clan list substitutes Elellee for Qaalluu ([Wikipedia: Ituu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ituu)); since the *Encyclopaedia Aethiopica* list contains both Elle and Qallu, the Wikipedia list is simply incomplete. The structure below the gosa matters for the book: Hararghe Oromo divide a gosa into sub-clans (ibidda) and then into ancestor-named kinship groups (warra), and "most of the Oromos of Harerghe are able to count down their forefathers up to the man" who founded their warra (oral tradition; [Afendi Muteki](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-oromo-of-harerghe-on-their-sub.html)). The notebook's own labels—"Qallu Dader," "Qalluu Abdusalamo," "Qallu warra biraa," "Warra Abdisabur/Mohammadnur," and the project's "Qallu Diini"—therefore read as several warra of one Qaalluu gosa living in and around Dadar, with Ali Shabel the eponym of one of them. **No published source names any Qaalluu warra, founder, or settlement**, so everything below the clan level rests on the notebook.

What kind of clan is it? The name is the ordinary Oromo word for a hereditary ritual leader, "a term for a sacred leader or priestly lineage" ([Wikipedia: Qallu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qallu)), and Nuraddin Aman's AAU thesis glossary simply defines "Warra Qāllū" as "Oromo priests" (documented; [AAU ETD](https://etd.aau.edu.et/bitstreams/b4e2ffef-bf77-42b6-88e4-6ff1f4c38834/download)). Among the Ittu, however, the documented bearers of the name are Muslim clerics. Braukämper (2002, p. 111, as relayed by Wikipedia) writes that "Somali-origin Warra Qallu missionaries subsequently established themselves among the Ituu in Chercher as Muslim religious leaders," that wherever they lived in an Oromo-speaking environment "they occupied a position as shaikhs and outstanding representatives of Muslim religion," and (p. 119) that they "preserved the memory of their Somali origin" (documented at second hand; [Wikipedia: Ituu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ituu)). The same author, cited at pp. 116–120, explains that the descendants of Sheikh Ali Jami near Gelemso are called "Qalicha" because of their Warra Qallu origin and "high priesthood" on the Chercher plateau ([Wikipedia: Galamso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galamso)). A Kiwix mirror of the Wikipedia "Qallu" article, again citing Braukämper (pp. 112–129), says the name is understood as "people of the religion," that most Qallu were "dominantly the teachers of Islam" where they lived, that they "trace back their genealogy to Abadir Umar Al Rida," and—most relevant here—that Qallu communities are "highly concentrated" in **Deder**, Babille, Jarso and Gursum ([Wikipedia: Qallu, Kiwix mirror](https://kaweah.freedombox.rocks/kiwix/wikipedia_en_all_maxi_2023-10/Qallu)). The only named individual explicitly "from the Qallu clan of the Ittu Oromo" in any source is Sheikh Bilal Kabir Muhammad, a qadi and teacher at Corree in Guba Qoricha and father of the famous Chercher scholar Sheikh Muhammad Rashid Bilal (1906/7–1988) (oral tradition; [Afendi Muteki](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/05/sheikh-muhammad-rashid-bilal-and-his.html)). The Dadar family's own clerical titles—Haajii Sheeh Mahammad, Sheik Abdulkerim Abrahim Hassan, Adame Qaadi, Aliya Habibe Kabira, Sitti Sheeka—fit this profile exactly.

The sources leave a genuine tension about origins that the book should present honestly rather than resolve. Braukämper's reading (documented, second hand) makes the Warra Qallu immigrants of Somali background who were absorbed into the Ittu. The Oromo tradition recorded at Odaa Bultum (documented in the Jimma thesis) makes the Warra Qallu an existing Ittu clan that did the absorbing: Oromo leaders asked Sheikh Abadir of Harar for a scholar, Aw Seid came, agreed to follow Oromo law and build a mosque, and "was officially adopted to the Oromo citizen and made 'ilma gosaa' (adopted son) of the Warra Qallu clan" and renamed Usso ([Admasu Feleke 2014](https://repository.ju.edu.et/bitstream/handle/123456789/1171/MAIN%20BODY%20OF%20ADMASU'S%20THESIS1.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y); [Wikipedia: Galamso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galamso)). Both can be true if "Qallu" was first the name of an Oromo priestly house into which Muslim holy men were adopted, after which the whole house came to be identified as the sheikh clan (inference). A third strand is Wikipedia-only: the article "Siddiqis in the Horn of Africa" states, without citation, that "Siddiqis in Eastern Ethiopia are usually called Qallu," claims descent from Abu Bakr through Abadir, and lists among "notable Siddiqi families" an **"Aw Qallu-Diinii family"** and an **"Aw Kurtu Dader family (in Harar, Dader and Djibouti)"** ([Wikipedia: Siddiqis in the Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddiqis_in_the_Horn_of_Africa)). These two entries are the closest published match to the notebook's "Qallu Diini" and "Qallu Dader" and they place such a family at Deder, but they are unsourced and may have been written by a member of that milieu (the list's one "notable Siddiqi," Ahmad Taqi, bears the same name as a son of Sheikh Muhammad Rashid Bilal). Treat them as leads to test with elders, not as facts. One further caution from the notebook itself: it records a man called "Diini (Dadar)" who married Faxuma Usmane Ali, a granddaughter of Ali Shabel. If the lineage name "Qallu Diini" derives from a person named Diini rather than from Arabic *dīn* ("religion"), then Ali Shabel would be Diini's father-in-law or kinsman rather than the lineage's apical ancestor—a question only elders can settle.

The geography is consistent with all of this. Dadar town "is basically attached to the Obbora clan of the Afran Qallo Oromo," sits next to the Ittu to the west and the Ala and Anniyya to the east and south, was "a center of Islamic education for a long time," and was capital of the Oborraa sub-province until 1991 (oral tradition; [Afendi Muteki, "Dadar"](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/05/dadar-colorful-town-in-east-hararge.html)). Biyyo Karaba is geocoded by a conflict-event database to about 9.37 N, 41.17 E in **Doba woreda, West Hararghe**, roughly 30 km west of Deder town and just across the zonal boundary ([CCAPS/ACLED location record](https://strauss.tacc.utexas.edu/ccaps/armedConflictLocation/show/5988016)); Doba is explicitly Chercher (Ittu) country ([Wikipedia: Chercher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chercher_(province))). A family spread over "Dadar and Biyyoo Karaabaa," with Sharifa households at Burqa and Ganda Ali, therefore straddles the very Burqa-river frontier that both the *Encyclopaedia Aethiopica* and Afendi Muteki name as the Ittu–Afran Qallo line (inference). The notebook's in-laws—Noolee, Jaarsoo, Oborra, Ituu, Somale, Abadir, Warra Garaaba—are the expected neighbours of such a frontier. Notably the notebook marks one spouse as "(Ituu)" as if Ittu were an outside clan, which suggests the Dadar Qaalluu may not think of themselves primarily as Ittu at all; the family's own answer to "are we Ittu, Oborra, or something else?" is one of the most valuable things an interview can record.

## Sharif lineages became Oromo warra by marriage and adoption

**No published or web source names Sharif Abdalla, Ammo Ali Shabel, Usman Darasa, Ganda Ali, or a Sharifa clan of Burqa**; the only "Abdallah Sharif" in Harar scholarship is a modern Harari manuscript collector catalogued by Betamasaheft, a different person entirely ([Betamasaheft IHA0554](https://betamasaheft.eu/manuscripts/IHA0554/text)). What the literature supplies is the template. Harar, the "City of Saints," received Arab holy men from the thirteenth century: Abadir ʿUmar ar-Riḍā is said to have arrived "from the Hijaz" in 612 H / 1216 AD with other religious leaders, to have "subsequently married a local Harari woman," and to be "the common ancestor of the Somali Sheekhaal clan and the Harari people" (documented via Wagner and Braukämper; [Wikipedia: Abadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadir_Umar_ar-Rida)); Insoll and Zekaria, quoting Braukämper, call him a "Yemeni Sufi Shaikh" who came "with a retinue of 405 Shaikhs" ([Insoll & Zekaria, Mosques of Harar](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/rest/bitstreams/155470/retrieve)). The Hijaz-versus-Yemen conflict in the sources is itself instructive: Arab holy-family origins in this region are claimed pedigrees, not records. A sixteenth-century "Fehkredin Yunis was the son of the Sherif of Mecca who came to Harar with the builders of the mosque" ([Insoll & Zekaria](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/rest/bitstreams/155470/retrieve)), and Harari society still reckons descent through patrilineal "houses" that include "Sharif Bitt'i, Sharif Kuta, Sharif Shilli" and "Arab Hassan, Arab Ali" ([Wikipedia: Harari people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harari_people)). The Sharifa of Dadar, with "Sharif" carried as a quasi-surname across five generations (Ali Sharif, Ahmed Sharif, Mumme Sharif, Mummad Baarun Ali Sharif, Abdi Yusuf Ali Sharif), are using exactly this Harari-style house label inside an Oromo-speaking community (inference).

The mechanism by which such a house becomes Oromo is documented in three steps. First, adoption: Gadaa law provided for "guddifachaa or amalgamation/moggaasaa of the non-Oromo to the Oromo" ([Admasu Feleke 2014](https://repository.ju.edu.et/bitstream/handle/123456789/1171/MAIN%20BODY%20OF%20ADMASU'S%20THESIS1.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)); Nuraddin Aman's glossary distinguishes guddifachaa "for a single individual" from moggaasa "for a given clan or group" and defines "ilma barč'uma" as "an adopted clan or individual" ([AAU ETD](https://etd.aau.edu.et/bitstreams/b4e2ffef-bf77-42b6-88e4-6ff1f4c38834/download)). Hararghe practice had a named category for exactly the sharif case: older outsiders admitted "due to their service to the society"—"the Ittu Oromo say 'ilma gosaa'," the Afran Qallo "ilma barcumaa"—who could vote but not hold office, and who "must practice an Oromo culture" and speak Afaan Oromoo (oral tradition; [Afendi Muteki](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/03/the-oromo-of-harerghe-on-their-sub.html)). That obligation is why the Sharifa speak Oromo and bear Oromo names. Second, marriage to a leading family: the best-documented Hararghe case is the "saintly" Sayyid family of the Abbu clan of Afran Qallo, ancestors of Sayyid Roba Garbi, where an Arsi chief "was compelled to give his daughter, Ayyo Sadiya to him" after a sign of the holy man's karama, and where another holy man, Sheikh Mohammed Alga, was "believed to be the legitimate descendant of Sof Umar and also the chief of Aymaro clan" ([Temam Hajiadem, Gadaa Journal 2020](https://ejhs.ju.edu.et/index.php/gadaa/issue/download/158/26)). Third, settlement and teaching: Islamic preachers "came with new house building and furnishing skills in addition to their religious knowledge," and their centres grew into Gelemso, Qunnii, Hirna, Watar and Babille (oral tradition; [Afendi Muteki](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/02/the-oromo-of-harerghe-on-evolution-of.html)). Sharif Abdalla marrying Ammo, daughter of the Qaalluu eponym, and his sons founding Ganda Ali and the Burqa households is the three-step template in miniature (inference).

The Aw Seid tradition deserves special attention because it is the single documented case of a *sharif* joining the *Qallu* of the Ittu. Aw Seid is "said to be a Sharif sent by Sheikh Abadir of Harar to preach around Gelemso," was made ilma gosa of the Warra Qallu, and "was given the name Usso"; Braukämper dates the mission to after the sixteenth century and the present shrine to the late nineteenth ([Wikipedia: Galamso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galamso); [Admasu Feleke 2014](https://repository.ju.edu.et/bitstream/handle/123456789/1171/MAIN%20BODY%20OF%20ADMASU'S%20THESIS1.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)). The notebook contains a Sharifa in-law family called Usso (Bakar Usso, who married Kadija Ali Sharif; Faxuma Bakar Usso; Massa Usso). Whether the Dadar Usso are connected to the Gelemso Aw Seid/Usso line is unknown and the name may be coincidence, but it is a lead worth one question to elders. The notebook's "Abrahim Hassan" family, recorded as Sharif "but not of Abdalla," matches what the literature predicts for a district like Dadar: several unrelated holy houses—Abadir-line, Sof Umar-line, Hijazi, Hadrami, Somali Sheekhaal—coexisting, each a separate warra (inference from [Temam Hajiadem 2020](https://ejhs.ju.edu.et/index.php/gadaa/issue/download/158/26) and [Wikipedia: Harari people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harari_people)). The notebook also records a "Sitti Sheeka (Abadir)" and an "Abadir Mohammad Haji," so an Abadir-claiming lineage already sits inside the family network, as the Qallu–Abadir literature would predict.

## The founders lived through conquest and conversion, not antiquity

Counting generations anchors the two founders in time. From Ali Shabel the notebook runs Habibe → Abdi → Haloo → Fatuma → Gutama → Fayo, six generations, and Abdi Habibe, with five wives and more than twenty children, must have been born around 1900 or a little earlier. At 25–30 years per generation, **Ali Shabel was an adult in roughly 1840–1870, and Sharif Abdalla married Ammo in roughly 1865–1895** (inference; the arithmetic is the report writer's, not any source's). That window is precisely when the Ittu became Muslim. Mohammed Hassen writes that "before the late 19th cent. few I[ttu] had converted to Islam" and that after the conquest of 1886 "the I. converted en masse, Islamic faith being for them a form of rejection of the gäbbar system" (documented; [EAe "Ittuu"](https://www.habeshahistory.com/encyclopedia/ittuu-p0272-b-y1277-v3)); Østebø dates the broader Islamisation to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries under Somali westward expansion, with Harari and Egyptian influence reinforcing it, and conversion around Hirna continued into the 1970s ([Wikipedia: Ituu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ituu)). A Muslim "priestly house" inside a still largely non-Muslim Ittu society before 1880 would have been a distinct minority, which is consistent with its being labelled with the priestly term qaalluu and with sharif families seeking it out for marriage (inference).

The political backdrop is well documented. Barentu Oromo reached the Harar plateau in the 1560s–1580s as the Adal sultanate collapsed; Emir Nur died in 1567 amid the famine that followed, and "by 1577 (the Oromo) had destroyed more than a hundred villages and besieged the city" ([Wikipedia: Oromo expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_expansion); [Wikipedia: Emirate of Harar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Harar)). The pre-Oromo Harla and Adal Muslim population was largely absorbed: "the Ittu Oromo (Chercher) incorporated the Harari and, plausibly, the Harla," and Christopher Ehret holds that "the greater part of the Adal Muslim population were assimilated by the Oromos" ([Wikipedia: Oromo expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_expansion); [Wikipedia: Harla people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harla_people)). Doba district itself is named as a site of Harla-related ruins ([Wikipedia: Chercher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chercher_(province))). Three centuries later the Egyptians occupied Harar (1875–1885), crushed the Afran Qallo under Orfo Jilo Biko, appointed Oromo garads and damins to administer the countryside, and ran proselytising campaigns ([Wikipedia: Egyptian invasion of Harar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_invasion_of_Harar)). Menelik invaded the Ittu from 1883, lost at Hirna in 1886, and won at Chelenqo on 9 January 1887 with 20,000–25,000 troops against 3,000–4,000 Harari; Ras Makonnen then expropriated land for his soldiers and a highland Christian elite became the rulers ([Wikipedia: Battle of Chelenqo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chelenqo); [Wikipedia: Ituu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ituu)). Dadar became one of Menelik's garrison towns, later the first town in Hararghe outside Harar and Dire Dawa to have a hospital, and the region's first high school (oral tradition; [Afendi Muteki, "Dadar"](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/05/dadar-colorful-town-in-east-hararge.html); [Afendi Muteki, urban centres](https://afendimutekiharar.blogspot.com/2013/02/the-oromo-of-harerghe-on-evolution-of.html)). Italian forces occupied Deder on 21 July 1936, and a Mennonite mission with school and hospital existed there by 1957 ([Wikipedia: Dader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dader)). Hararghe province was created in 1943 and divided among Oromia, Somali, Afar and Harari regions in 1995 ([Wikipedia: Hararghe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hararghe)). The notebook's scatter of residences—Dire Dawa (a railway town), Adama, Harar, Ciro, Addis, Saudi Arabia, Canada, America, Australia—traces the twentieth-century labour, pilgrimage and refugee flows that followed; no source quantifies the Hararghe diaspora specifically, so the book should describe this through family testimony.

The Ittu's own institutions also frame the story. Odaa Bultum near Baddessa was "the common assembly of the three tribes of the Harar Oromo" (Ittu, Afran Qallo, Anniya); its Caffee met every eight years and elected the Abbaa Gadaa and five ministers, and in the later nineteenth century the war council (Raabaa Doorii) grew dominant until "up on the arrival of foreign invaders, their ancient and democratic administrative system totally collapsed" (oral tradition citing Mohammed Hassen; [Afendi Muteki, Oda Bultum](https://maddawalaabuupress.blogspot.com/2013/01/the-oromo-of-harergheon-tradition-of.html)). The Ittu's pre-Islamic worship was conducted "under a big oak tree by the side of Hirna River ... at a place called Hujjuba" ([Wikipedia: Ituu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ituu)). The Dadar Qaalluu would have lived under the last functioning gadaa generations and the first imperial governors; whether any ancestor held a gadaa office, served as a garad or damin under the Egyptians, or was a balabbat under Menelik is an answerable question for elders.

## Two marriage codes explain the notebook's web of cousins

Classical Oromo kinship rests on "a strict prohibition of clan endogamy"; cousin marriage is "in complete contradiction with Oromo kinship system," and relatives are "those whose seven or less ancestors converge" (documented; [DICE Missouri Oromo profile 2](https://dice.missouri.edu/assets/docs/afro-asiatic/Oromo2.pdf)). In Salale, the groom's family checks back seven generations for shared blood, and marriage forms include *kadhaa/naqataa* (formal request), *butii* (abduction), *aseennaa* (the woman presents herself), *dhaala* (levirate) and *hirpha* (sororate) ([Tigist Moreda, AAU 2008](https://etd.aau.edu.et/bitstreams/11b81265-400b-49c7-ac16-98581ee1617a/download)). **No fetched source describes Ittu or Afran Qallo marriage rules specifically**, so the Hararghe application below is inference. Islamic law, by contrast, permits first-cousin marriage, and Harar's Muslim elite intermarried with Oromo from early on: "marriages with Oromo women were frequent" under the early emirs and Emir Abdallah ibn Ali "had two wives of Oromo heritage" ([Wikipedia: Emirate of Harar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Harar)).

Read against both codes, the notebook's roughly forty starred marriages fall into two kinds. The first and largest kind is **exchange between two distinct gosa**: a Sharifa man marrying a Qaalluu woman or the reverse, repeated in every generation (Mummad Ammade Ali Shabel and Khadija Ahmed Sharif; Ibro Usmane Ali Shabel and Fatuma Ali Sharif; Mumme Ibro Ali Shabel and Mardiya Ali Sharif; Abrahim Habibe Ali Shabel and Zabida Ahmed Sharif; Adurman Sharif and Zeyno Habibe Ali Shabel). Because Oromo descent is patrilineal and the prohibition is on the father's gosa, every one of these is exogamous by Oromo rule even though the two founders were father-in-law and son-in-law; repeated alliance between two allied clans is a recognised Oromo pattern and would look like "cousin marriage" only in a bilateral chart (inference). The second kind shows the Islamic code at work. Jawad Ahmed Aderman (Sharifa by father, but his mother Amina Yuyya Usmane was a granddaughter of Usmane Ali Shabel) married Haloya Mummad Adame, whose father Mummad Adame Usmane was that same Usmane's grandson: patrilineally a Sharifa–Qaalluu match, but matrilaterally the pair were second cousins, closer than any strict seven-generation Oromo check would allow and entirely ordinary under Islamic law. The same holds for Umar Abibakar and Miski Abdalla Mumme Abdi, or Nuure Mummad Baarun and Sadiya Abrahim Mumme Ibro. The practical conclusion for the book is that by the twentieth century these families reckoned marriageability by patrilineal gosa plus Islamic law, not by the old seven-generation rule—a shift no Hararghe source documents but the notebook itself demonstrates.

Three further patterns in the notebook have documented explanations. Polygyny is heavy in the senior generations (Abdi Habibe Ali Shabel with five wives; Mumme Abdi with three; several men with a noted "second wife"), and the Oromo profile links polygyny among some men to "conversion to Islam and other cultural influences" ([DICE Missouri Oromo profile 2](https://dice.missouri.edu/assets/docs/afro-asiatic/Oromo2.pdf)). The notebook's doubled names—Nafisa Abdi *guddoo* and Nafisa Abdi *xiqqo*, both daughters of the five-wived Abdi Habibe—are the expected product of co-wives naming daughters after the same grandmother (inference; the Hararghe gloss of guddoo/xiqqoo is unverified, see glossary). Levirate is explicit: *dhaala* is attested as a marriage form (marriage to the deceased husband's brother) in Salale ([Tigist Moreda, AAU 2008](https://etd.aau.edu.et/bitstreams/11b81265-400b-49c7-ac16-98581ee1617a/download)), and the notebook's "ni dhaale" and "imama dhaale" annotations against widows (Khadija Ahmed Sharif, Aisha Dawud, Abdalla Abibakar Yonis) almost certainly mark such inheritances, which Islamic law also allows after the waiting period; against a child the same verb can mean "was taken in" (inference). Several women are recorded with second husbands (Aisha Ibro, Marjane Umar Ali Sharif, Halo Ahmed Abibe, Aliya Habibe Ahmed), which is consistent with widow remarriage under both codes. Finally, the Oromo themselves "recognize social ancestry" and do not sharply separate biological from social descent, so a clan "need not all be Oromo" ([DICE Missouri Oromo profile](https://dice.missouri.edu/assets/docs/afro-asiatic/Oromo.pdf)); a family book that lists adopted and inherited members alongside birth members is following Oromo convention, not departing from it.

## No register names rural Hararghe families before 2004

The archival picture is stark. "Civil registration as a source of vital statistics had never existed in Ethiopia" before the 2012 law; only municipalities of big towns issued fragmentary certificates, and the statistical agency ran sample studies from the 1970s ([UNECA CRVS country report](https://apai-crvs.uneca.org/sites/default/files/resourcefiles/Country%20Report%20-%20Ethiopia_Eng_0.pdf)). Nationwide registration of births, deaths, marriages and divorces "began on Saturday, 6 August 2016," with kebele managers as registrars and certificates issued at registration ([UN Ethiopia 2016](https://ethiopia.un.org/en/14847-vital-events-registration-kicks-ethiopia); [UNICEF CRVS profile](https://data.unicef.org/crvs/ethiopia/)); records are paper-based at kebele level, late registration exists, and even now birth registration is only about **37 percent complete** ([Capital Ethiopia, Nov 2025](https://capitalethiopia.com/2025/11/09/digital-dreams-invisible-children-ethiopias-struggle-to-make-every-birth-count/)). The Fayda digital ID, launched 2024 with 12 million enrolled by February 2025, is an identity credential, not a genealogical record ([World Bank 2025](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2025/02/27/the-transformative-power-of-ethiopia-afe-digital-id-unlocking-a-better-future-for-all)). For anyone who died before 2016, in other words, the Ethiopian state holds no vital record.

The one name-level paper source for the living and recently deceased is **rural land certification**. Oromia implemented first-level certification from 2004 under Proclamation 56/2002; in surveyed districts 51–56 percent of certificates carried "husband, wife and children's names," the husband's photograph appeared on 87–93 percent, polygamous households received joint or per-wife certificates, and kebele Land Administration Committees compiled the books ([UN-HABITAT, Land Registration in Ethiopia](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/781575/files/Land%2520Registration%2520in%2520Ethiopia%2520Early%2520Impacts%2520on%2520Women.pdf)). The survey districts were in West Arsi, not Hararghe, so the exact years and format for Dadar and Doba are inferred from the Oromia-wide rollout. A kebele's land book is effectively a census of landholding households around 2004–2010 with spouses named, and since Biyyo Karaba falls in Doba woreda (West Hararghe) while Dadar town and its kebeles fall in East Hararghe, records must be sought at both woreda land offices ([CCAPS/ACLED record](https://strauss.tacc.utexas.edu/ccaps/armedConflictLocation/show/5988016); [Wikipedia: Doba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doba_(woreda)); [Wikipedia: Dadar (Aanaa)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadar_(Aanaa))). Census data exist only in aggregate or anonymised form: Dadar woreda had 179,541 people in 1994 (91.86 percent Muslim, 92.12 percent Oromo) and 242,140 in 2007; Doba had 92,003 in 1994 (86.14 percent Muslim, 88.88 percent Oromo) and 133,939 in 2007 ([Wikipedia: Dadar (Aanaa)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadar_(Aanaa)); [Wikipedia: Doba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doba_(woreda))), and IPUMS holds 10 percent samples of the 1984, 1994 and 2007 censuses with woreda as the smallest geography, useful for describing household structure but never for finding a person ([IPUMS International: Ethiopia](https://international.ipums.org/international-action/sample_details/country/et)). Whether the original enumeration forms survive at the Ethiopian Statistics Service is unconfirmed.

Older state records are thin and uncatalogued. The Italian Harar Governorate (1936–41) had a "Cercer" commissariat covering Chercher ([Wikipedia: Harar Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harar_Governorate)); Michigan State University holds "Governo del Harar" finding aids that could not be opened in this research ([MSU finding aids](https://findingaids.lib.msu.edu/repositories/4/archival_objects/413911)), and any tax or chief lists would be in Rome or on MSU microfilm. The National Archives and Library of Ethiopia, with archives established in 1979, lists no Hararghe provincial files online ([Wikipedia: NALA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archives_and_Library_of_Ethiopia)). Imperial land-tax rolls (1942–74) and Derg peasant-association lists (1975–91), if they survive, sit uncatalogued in woreda offices (inference). FamilySearch operates a centre in Addis Ababa but no Ethiopian record collection surfaced, and with no church-register tradition in Muslim Hararghe nothing for Dadar is expected ([FamilySearch catalog](https://familysearch.org/en/search/catalog)). The genuinely promising written tradition is local and Arabic: Shaykh Bakri Sapalo (Abū-Bakr ʿUthmān) of Hararghe wrote a *Bayān Silsilat Nasab al-Gāllā*, "Explaining the [Oromo] genealogy," edited and translated with a chapter on the "Genealogical setting of the Hararghe Oromo" in Nuraddin Aman's 2020 AAU doctoral thesis ([AAU ETD record](https://etd.aau.edu.et/items/3b06c790-80ff-464b-acd0-cd8106b7270e)); only its front matter could be read here. Sheikhly families of the Horn commonly keep such silsila sheets, and the University of Copenhagen's Islam in the Horn of Africa project catalogues Arabic manuscript fragments from the region ([IslHornAfr database](https://islhornafr.tors.sc.ku.dk/backend/manuscript_fragments/9771)). For the Qaalluu and Sharifa, the family's own sheikhs are the most likely custodians of a written nasab.

Four offices hold the only paper that can add names. The Dadar woreda Land Administration and Use office and the kebele Land Administration Committees for the family's kebeles hold the first-level land books from about 2004, which name husband, wife and often children; the Doba woreda land office and the Biyyo Karaba kebele hold the same for the western branch; the kebele civil registration desks in both woredas hold births, marriages and deaths since August 2016 and can accept late registrations with evidence; and the woreda administration may still hold Derg-era peasant-association rolls or imperial tax lists, which no one has inventoried. A relative on site should ask the kebele manager and the LAC chair directly; nothing is reachable by web request. In the diaspora, two further checks cost little: a FamilySearch place search for "Ethiopia" (free login) to rule the catalogue out formally, and an email to the MSU Libraries Africana curator asking whether the "Governo del Harar" microfilm includes Cercer commissariat lists of chiefs and taxpayers for Deder or Doba.

The questions for elders should be designed to test the hypotheses above rather than merely to collect names. Ask what "Qaalluu" means in their own usage: the Ittu gosa, the sheikh lineage ("Qallu Diini"), or both, and whether the family considers itself Ittu, Oborra/Afran Qallo, or neither. Ask who Diini was—whether "Qallu Diini" is named after a man called Diini (the notebook records a Diini of Dadar married to Faxuma Usmane Ali) or means "Qallu of the religion"—and how he relates to Ali Shabel. Ask whether the family remembers a Somali, Harari, Arab or Abadir origin for the Qaalluu, and whether the sheikhs of the family hold an Arabic silsila or nasab sheet; request a photograph of any such document. Ask where Sharif Abdalla came from (Harar, Yemen, Hijaz, Somali country, another Oromo district), who his father was, whether he was a sheikh, qadi or teacher, whether he was formally "moggaafame" or made "ilma gosaa," and what land or role he received when he married Ammo. Ask whether the Usso family of Bakar Usso is connected to the Aw Seid/Usso lineage of Gelemso. Ask for the names of the other Qaalluu warra (Abdusalamo, Abdisabur/Mohammadnur, "warra biraa") and their eponyms, and whether any ancestor held a gadaa office, served as garad or damin under the Egyptians, or as balabbat after Menelik. Ask which graves and shrines (awach) in Dadar, Biyyo Karaba, Burqa and Ganda Ali belong to the founders, since shrines carry names and approximate dates. Finally, record each elder's own age and parentage, because the elder's position in the tree is itself a datum that fixes the chronology.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the book's two founders. Ali Shabel is not the origin of the Qaalluu but a nineteenth-century eponym within a clan already old enough to sit in the *Encyclopaedia Aethiopica*'s Ittu clan lists and already Muslim and clerical enough to be called "the priests"; Sharif Abdalla is one of several holy-family men who, in the generation of Egyptian and Shewan conquest, attached themselves to that clerical house through marriage and adoption. The book's genuine contribution is therefore not to trace either man to Arabia or Somalia—no document will do that—but to record, before the last elders who knew the grandchildren die, how a Qaalluu house and a Sharifa house on the Burqa frontier wove themselves into a single Oromo-speaking community through forty cross-marriages, levirate, polygyny and shared Islamic learning.

Two findings should change how the project proceeds. First, the "Aw Qallu-Diinii" and "Aw Kurtu Dader" entries, the Aw Seid/Usso tradition, and the Abadir claims are leads that the family's own sheikhs can confirm or refute in a single conversation, which makes elder interviews and the hunt for a family Arabic silsila more valuable than any archive trip. Second, because the only name-level government records are the post-2004 land books and post-2016 civil registers, the genealogy's verification for anyone born before about 1980 will rest entirely on triangulating independent oral accounts; the notebook should be treated as one witness, and each branch re-collected from at least one elder of a different warra.
